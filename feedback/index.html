Feedback

Part way through the module, and again at the end of it, I give over part of a class to anonymous feedback: what is working, what is not, and anything else you want to tell me. Every round is kept on this page, newest first, exactly as it was written and with my response to it, so the record builds up year on year.

I publish it for two reasons. The first is that you can see the feedback is read and acted on rather than collected and filed. The second is that the comments tell you what the people who sat in your seat found hard, which is useful when you are working out how to study.

Responses appear as they were submitted, including the typos. A few are cut short because the polling tool limits how much can be typed into one answer.

Mid-module feedback, 2026/27

2026-10-23 · 138 responses

Collected in class on 23 October 2026, in week 4 of the module. Fifty-seven of you answered the first question, 39 the second and 17 the third. Thank you.

What works

Forty-seven of the 73 comments on what works name the activities: the games keep the room awake, and several of you say that playing a game first and meeting the theory afterwards helps with the theory. Twenty-eight mention the website, the notes, the worked solutions or the video walkthroughs. The enthusiasm, the questions in class and the bribentives all get a mention too. None of that is changing.

What does not work

One theme dominates, and it is a fair one. Thirty of the 47 comments on what has not worked are about the mathematical content: the definitions, the theorems and the notation in the notes, how much of them appears in class, and how clearly the game we have just played connects to them. Several of you put it as "sometimes it feels like we just play games", and several more said that the subgame perfection class, where we played the Traitors and then wrote the centipede game out on the board, was a good mix.

Twelve comments ask for more exam-style work in class and a clearer statement of which proofs and definitions are examinable. Six are about the games themselves, mostly that the rules are not clear enough when you play in pairs. Five ask for resources: the slides, a version of the notes you can download and annotate, more past papers, and better handwriting in the videos. Three are about the room, which I will get back to below.

What I am doing

  1. Every class will end on the notes. Each slide carries the numbered section of the textbook it is reading, and the last slide will summarise them into one table, marks what is examinable, and says what to read before the next class.
  2. More of each class will be spent turning the game into notation, the way the subgame perfection class did.
  3. When an examinable theorem comes up, I will say so in class and point at the list of examinable theorems.
  4. The slide decks are on the site already, linked at the foot of every topic page, so you can read them before class and keep them afterwards. I will not be uploading the versions I annotate live.
  5. I will add a summary of rules of each game slide to stay on screen while you play it, so you are not working from memory once you are in pairs.
  6. The notes are linked as a PDF you can download and annotate, at the foot of every topic page.
  7. Last year's exam paper is on the Assessment page.
