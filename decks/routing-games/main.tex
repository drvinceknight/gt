\slidehead{Phase 1: is anybody still moving?}
\notesref{14.2.6}{Routing Games: Definition: Nash Flow}

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\large Where we settled: \answerblank[1.4]{\(20\)} on top,
\answerblank[1.4]{\(20\)} on bottom, both taking \answerblank[1.4]{\(70\)}
minutes.}

\vspace{0.6cm}

{\large In the room, one driver who switches makes their new road one car busier,
so their time goes from \answerblank[1.4]{\(70\)} to \answerblank[1.4]{\(71\)}.}

\vspace{0.6cm}

{\large In the model, each of us is a tiny share of a flow of 40 drivers: the
traffic is \textbf{non-atomic}. One driver switching changes no road's time, so
the only question is whether some other route is already quicker.}

\vspace{0.6cm}

{\large\bfseries So this is a \answerrule[5cm]{Nash} flow.}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\small\color{inkgrey} A flow is a Nash flow when:}\\[0.4em]
\answerspace{22.6}{2.2}{%
  Every route that carries traffic takes the least time: if \(f_{P_1} > 0\) then
  \(c_{P_1}(f) \leq c_{P_2}(f)\), with both costs worked out at the same flow
  \(f\).}

\newpage
\slidehead{Why it got worse}
\notesref{14.2.9}{Routing Games: Definition: Marginal Cost}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\large Add a sliver \(\delta\) of traffic to a small road that already carries
\(x\).}

\vspace{0.7cm}

\hspace{1cm}
\begin{minipage}{0.9\linewidth}
  {\large The sliver itself spends \answerblank[1.4]{\(x\)} minutes per driver:
   \answerblank[1.4]{\(x\delta\)} in all.}\\[0.9em]
  {\large The \(x\) already there are each slowed by \answerblank[1.4]{\(\delta\)}:
   \answerblank[1.4]{\(x\delta\)} in all.}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.7cm}

{\large A driver feels the first and ignores the second. The road's total time is}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{center}
{\Large $x \, c(x) \;=\;$ \answerblank[2]{\(x^{2}\)} \hspace{2.2cm}
 and the sliver really adds
 $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\big(x \, c(x)\big)\,\delta \;=\;$
 \answerblank[2]{\(2x\)}$\,\delta$}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{center}
\answerspace{22.6}{2.3}{%
  Each driver counts the \(x\) minutes they spend and none of the delay they put
  on the traffic already there. At \(x = 40\), more traffic really costs \(80\)
  minutes per driver added against the \(40\) that driver feels, which is the whole of
  the gap between the Nash flow and the optimal one.}
\end{center}

\newpage
\slidehead{Two theorems}
\notesref{14.2.7 to 14.2.10}{Routing Games: Potential Function, Marginal Cost}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\tikz\node[draw=inkteal, line width=1pt, rounded corners=4pt, fill=white,
  text width=10.7cm, inner sep=0.45cm, align=left, text=inktext]{%
  {\bfseries\color{inkteal} Theorem.} A Nash flow minimises the potential
  \[
    \Phi(f) = \sum_{e} \int_{0}^{f_e} c_e(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
  \]};

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\large All 40 on the new route: \(\Phi =\) \answerblank[1.8]{\(1600\)}}

\vspace{0.6em}

{\large Twenty each way: \(\Phi =\) \answerblank[1.8]{\(2400\)}}

\vspace{0.6em}

{\large\color{inkgrey} The room minimised \(\Phi\), not the time. An
integral, not a sum: the traffic is non-atomic.}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\tikz\node[draw=inkred, line width=1pt, rounded corners=4pt, fill=white,
  text width=10.7cm, inner sep=0.45cm, align=left, text=inktext]{%
  {\bfseries\color{inkred} Theorem.} An optimal flow is a Nash flow for the
  marginal costs
  \[
    c^{*}_e(x) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\big(x\,c_e(x)\big).
  \]};

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\large Small road \answerblank[1.4]{\(2x\)} \quad motorway
\answerblank[1.4]{\(50\)} \quad new road \answerblank[1.4]{\(0\)}}

\vspace{0.6em}

{\large Equal marginal times when \answerblank[1.2]{\(10\)} take the new
road and \answerblank[1.2]{\(15\)} take each other route.}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\begin{center}
\answerspace{22.6}{2.2}{%
  Small roads give \(\int_0^{40} x\,\mathrm{d}x = 800\) each, so
  \(\Phi = 1600\). With 10, 15 and 15, every route costs \(100\) in
  marginal minutes: \(2 \times 25 + 0 + 2 \times 25 = 2 \times 25 + 50\).}
\end{center}

\newpage
\slidehead{What it costs us all}
\notesref{14.2.4, 14.2.5}{Routing Games: Cost function, Optimal Flow}

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\large The total time of a flow \(f\) adds up every road: \quad
\(\displaystyle C(f) = \sum_{e} f_e\, c_e(f_e)\)}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}
{\large Left alone, the Nash flow \(\tilde f\):}\\[0.5em]
\answerspace{10.8}{3.4}{%
  All 40 on the new route:
  \(C(\tilde f) = 40 \times 40 + 40 \times 0 + 40 \times 40 = 3200\).}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}
{\large The optimal flow \(f^{*}\), from the second theorem:}\\[0.5em]
\answerspace{10.8}{3.4}{%
  25 on each small road, 15 on each motorway:
  \(C(f^{*}) = 2 \times 25^{2} + 2 \times 15 \times 50 = 2750\).}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.8cm}

\begin{center}
{\Large Price of anarchy \(\;=\; \dfrac{C(\tilde f)}{C(f^{*})} \;=\;
 \dfrac{\;\answerblank[1.8]{\(3200\)}\;}%
 {\;\answerblank[1.8]{\(2750\)}\;} \;=\;\)
 \answerblank[2]{\(64/55\)}}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.4cm}

{\small\color{inkgrey} Twenty each way, ignoring the new road, costs 2800: better
than the room, but not the best a planner can do.}
