\newpage
\slidehead{So there is no pure equilibrium}
\notesref{5.2.1}{Nash Equilibrium: Definition: Nash Equilibria}

\vspace{0.6cm}

{\large Whatever the pair of actions, somebody wants to move:}

\vspace{0.8cm}

\hspace{1cm}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\large]
  \node[anchor=west] at (0,0)
    {If they play Rock, I want \answerblank[2.6]{Paper}};
  \node[anchor=west] at (11,0)
    {If they play Paper, I want \answerblank[2.6]{Scissors}};
  \node[anchor=west] at (0,-1.6)
    {If they play Scissors, I want \answerblank[2.6]{Rock}};
  \node[anchor=west] at (11,-1.6)
    {\ldots\ and then they want to move too.};
\end{tikzpicture}

\vspace{0.9cm}

{\large\bfseries So any equilibrium has to be
\answerrule[4.5cm]{mixed}.}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{center}
\answerspace{22.6}{2.6}{%
  Whichever cell we point at, one of the two players is losing or drawing and
  would rather move to the action that beats what the other is doing. No pair
  of pure actions survives, so nothing on the grid can be an equilibrium.}
\end{center}

\newpage
\slidehead{The best response condition}
\notesref{3.2.6}{Rationalisation: Theorem: Best Response Condition}

\vspace{0.5cm}

{\large A mixed strategy \(\sigma\) is a best response to \(\tau\) if and only
if}

\vspace{0.6cm}

\begin{center}
\answerspace{21}{3.4}{%
  \[
    \sigma_i > 0 \implies u_r(i, \tau)
      = \max_{k} u_r(k, \tau) \quad \text{for every action } i.
  \]}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.9cm}

{\large In words: every action I actually use has to be
\answerrule[6cm]{a best response}.}

\vspace{0.9cm}

{\large So if I mix over all three actions, all three must give me the same
expected payoff.}

\newpage
\slidehead{Making the other player indifferent}
\notesref{3.2.6}{Rationalisation: Theorem: Best Response Condition}

\vspace{0.3cm}

{\large Suppose the column player uses
\(\sigma_c = (x, y, 1 - x - y)\) over (Rock, Paper, Scissors).}

\vspace{0.6cm}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.52\linewidth}
{\large
\begin{tabbing}
\(u_r(\text{Scissors}, \sigma_c) \;=\;\) \= \kill
\(u_r(\text{Rock}, \sigma_c) \;=\;\) \>
  \answerblank[5.4]{\(1 - x - 2y\)}\\[1.5em]
\(u_r(\text{Paper}, \sigma_c) \;=\;\) \>
  \answerblank[5.4]{\(2x + y - 1\)}\\[1.5em]
\(u_r(\text{Scissors}, \sigma_c) \;=\;\) \>
  \answerblank[5.4]{\(y - x\)}
\end{tabbing}}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.44\linewidth}
{\small\color{inkgrey} Set them equal, and solve:}\\[0.4em]
\answerspace{10.2}{5.2}{%
  The first and the third agree when \(1 - x - 2y = y - x\), so
  \(y = 1/3\). The second and the third agree when
  \(2x + y - 1 = y - x\), so \(x = 1/3\). All three are then \(0\).}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.8cm}

\begin{center}
{\Large \(\sigma_c \;=\;\) \answerblank[6]{\large\(\left(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13\right)\)}}
\end{center}
