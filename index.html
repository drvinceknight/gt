FAQs

Can I succeed in this course even if I am unsure about my programming ability?

Yes. There is no programming required for this course and none of it is assessed. The textbook includes optional Python for those who want to explore the ideas computationally, but you do not need to write or run any code to do well.

I see a lot of Python in the textbook. Is it needed for this course?

No. There is no programming required for this module: it is not assessed and you do not need to write or run any code to do well. The Python in the textbook is entirely optional. It is there so you can explore the ideas computationally and check your working if you would like to, but you are welcome to ignore it.

If you do want to try it and are not familiar with Python, these chapters of Python For Mathematics on installing and using Python and installing and using other libraries are a good place to start.

Can I use AI tools such as ChatGPT or Claude to help me study?

You may, but I encourage you to be deliberate about how. The assessment for this course is a closed-book examination with no generative AI allowed, so the only thing that matters on the day is what you can do yourself.

The evidence so far is that these tools help or harm depending on how they are used. In a field experiment with nearly a thousand mathematics students published in PNAS, students with unrestricted access to GPT-4 did much better on practice problems, but once access was removed they did worse on the exam than students who had never used it. Notably, a version of the tutor constrained to give hints rather than answers removed most of that harm.

So if you use an LLM, use it in ways that keep you doing the thinking: ask it to quiz you on the definitions, ask it for a hint rather than a solution, or show it an attempt you have already written and ask it to find the error. Do not ask it to answer an example question before you have attempted the question in full yourself. Otherwise you are practising for an examination that the AI sits, and not the one that you will.

Why is the assessment entirely exam based, with no generative AI allowed?

The aim of the course is that you can do game theory: state and prove the results, compute the equilibria and interpret what they mean. A closed-book examination is the most direct and fair way to assess exactly that, and it is a deliberate choice meant to protect your learning.

Recent published research shows that passing your thinking on to an AI weakens the very abilities a course is meant to build. A study in PNAS found that students with access to GPT-4 improved on practice problems but performed worse than their peers once access was removed: the tool did the learning instead of them. A study of 666 participants found that heavier AI use was associated with weaker critical thinking, explained by cognitive offloading, the habit of handing thinking to the tool rather than doing it. And a brain-imaging study at MIT found that essay writers using an LLM showed the weakest neural connectivity and the poorest recall of their own work, an effect the authors call accumulating 'cognitive debt'. None of this is entirely new: a classic analysis by Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argued two decades ago that learning is a change in long-term memory, and that teaching approaches fail when they leave the learner consulting a source instead of building knowledge of their own. In short, thinking works like a muscle: the skills you hand over, you lose.

An examination with no generative AI means the work you put in during the course, attempting the example questions and reproducing the proofs yourself, is precisely the work that is rewarded. You will certainly use AI tools in your working life, but they are most valuable to people who understand the material well enough to direct them and to catch their mistakes; that understanding is what this course, and its examination, are for.

What is a bribentive?

A bribentive is a word we use in class for the small rewards handed out during some activities. It is a blend of bribe and incentive: the reward is usually just a sweet or a chocolate bar, so it is not really a good incentive, more a small bribe for engagement. The games still work without it, but it adds a bit of fun to the competition.
